The moderator is Motunrayo Opayinka (Womenovate). Fellow panelists include Alex Tsado (Udutech), Ashley Immanuel (Semicolon), Lanre Ogundipe (Imperial Edtech), and Prof. Chika Yinka Banjo (UNILAG). 1
How do you design your robotics training programs to catalyse quick employment for graduates?

RAIN is a unique institution — not a university, not a secondary school. It is a school built specifically to fill the skills gap that society has left open in robotics. We are deliberate about this. We train for outcomes: to build opportunities, access jobs, and create an advanced society.

Our curriculum does two things. First, it covers every knowledge area a robotics engineer must have — from 3D design and programming, to web development, embedded systems, IoT, cybersecurity, and desktop development, all the way through to the core of robotics: SLAM, path planning, navigation, PID control, robot arm control, and drone development. We understood early that robotics is not a standalone field.

Second, we build careers — not just competencies. Using platforms like LinkedIn, we ensure trainees put themselves out there. Opportunities don't come looking for you. You have to be visible.

"At RAIN, it is not what you know — it is what you can do."

Projects are central from day one. We provide the kits, the workshop, the laboratory, and the parts, so trainees have no excuse but to build. We have had mechanical engineering graduates who only found confidence in calling themselves engineers after RAIN — because here, they were made to do advanced practical work they would otherwise have outsourced.

2
Where have you seen robotics generate measurable productivity gains — and what evidence should funders look for?

Robotics is not just about replacing humans in dangerous tasks — it is about freeing people. A person who would have stood by the roadside to sell goods can now deploy a robot to do that while they manage from a distance. That is empowerment, not displacement.

The area I point to for measurable productivity is smart vending. Go to Dubai, go to the US — in train stations and subways, vending machines are generating money for owners around the clock with no one there. They squeeze orange juice, dispense drinks, detect faults, and self-report issues. It works.

In Nigeria, we can domesticate this. Imagine an akara vending machine at the airport, at a shopping mall, at a train station — hygienic, consistent, smart. This is a low-hanging fruit that investors should take seriously. Start in supermarkets and restaurants, validate the model, then scale out.

Funders should look for: cost-per-unit reduction, revenue generated per hour without human supervision, and consistency of output quality. Those are the metrics that matter.

3
What is one low-cost robotics application for fast pilots with immediate ROI?

My answer here connects to Question 2 — what generates income is what we should recommend, because it has been tested.

Smart vending remains the strongest recommendation: domesticated, locally adapted vending machines serving Nigerian street food and beverages. It is tangible, it is profitable, and it generates immediate public trust in robotics.

People talk about drones, autonomous vehicles, and humanoid robots — and these are exciting areas — but they are still in research phase. Nobody is making money from fully autonomous vehicles yet. The vending machine is real, it is working, and it can work here.

4
What are the 1–2 most viable pathways for a robotics research lab to transition into a scalable product or startup?

It is no longer business as usual. The era of academics producing miniature robot cars is over. What can a 12x18cm robot do for Nigeria?

Pathway one: bring artisans into the lab. A welder, a mechanical fitter, an electrician, a blacksmith — these are the people who will take your beautiful code and turn it into something rugged, industrial, and real. At RAIN, we built Unmanned Ground Vehicles using locally fabricated materials from the welder's workshop. We are currently recruiting artisans for this exact reason.

Pathway two: fund boldly and collaborate fearlessly. Labs must take financial risk. Collaborate with fabricators and industry players. Open apprenticeship opportunities — at RAIN, we have apprenticeship openings precisely because we know the next generation of robotics entrepreneurs must learn in that hybrid environment.

The medical sector demands robotic arms in stainless steel — not 3D-printed plastic. Drones must be weather-proofed. Labs that face these industrial realities head-on are the ones that will produce scalable products.

5
What are the most significant technical bottlenecks African robotics engineers face that require localized R&D?

I will not say that sensors made abroad don't work in Nigeria. They do — manufacturers make products for every climate, every pressure range, every price point. The problem is not the sensors; it is what we can afford, what we can access, and what we are producing ourselves.

The real bottleneck is that we are not producing our own components. Because demand is low and funding is scarce, importers bring in cheap, low-quality stock. The market is self-defeating.

My prescription: young engineers must choose a specialisation within the supply chain and become unit experts. If it is motors — understand gears, torque, and fabricate your own. If it is sensors — study the chemistry and physics and build your own. If it is battery packs — Aba should be producing battery packs of different specifications so that when you are building a robot, you call a local supplier, not Alibaba.

We may never produce our own microchips — but if we can produce everything else, we will have built a self-sufficient ecosystem. Right now, robotics in Nigeria is largely personally funded by individuals. Large companies are not buying Nigerian-made robots. That must change — and it starts with us building the supply chain from within.

6
What is your vision for a national robotics research and prototyping facility that serves both academia and industry?

I must make a caveat: centralisation is no longer the answer. We used to say united we stand, divided we fall — but we are entering an era where, if done right, united we fall and divided we stand.

division
The vision lives inside the division

The war between Iran, the US, and Israel has taught everyone — including the US — a serious lesson. Iran survived and remained operational because it had multiple distributed command centres. There was no single point that could be taken out. And because of this, many countries are now rethinking their structures. We will see significant changes globally in how nations organise their command, defence, and critical infrastructure going forward.

Nigeria is already beginning to reflect this thinking — the growing seriousness around state police is evidence of decentralisation arriving at our own doorstep. Power is being pushed outward, not held at the centre.

Now apply that same logic to robotics. In network topology we have master-to-slave and peer-to-peer. With master-to-slave, once the master is corrupted, everybody is in trouble. But with peer-to-peer, anybody can assume coordination. Blockchain is the proof of concept — everybody is a server, nobody is the lord, everybody is a server, nobody is a servant. That is the architecture we need for national robotics infrastructure.

My vision is a distributed national robotics ecosystem — not one central facility that can be starved of funding or politicised. Instead: somewhere in the North, a centre building battery technology from our lithium deposits. Somewhere in the South, a centre producing sensors. In the East, excellence in embedded systems. In the West, specialists in long-range communication systems.

Each node is independent, but they share a common vision. When one falls, the others stand.

"The vision is intact as long as the division does not destroy the vision."

Government must encourage, fund, and coordinate this distributed structure — not build one central facility and call it done.
