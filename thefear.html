The RAIN Communiqué

Ibadan, Nigeria 11 September 2026 Open Seminar Proceedings

Members of Robotics and Artificial Intelligence Nigeria, developers under the certification programme, faculty, staff, and principal officials alike, gathered in Ibadan for an unscripted, wide-ranging seminar on whether artificial intelligence poses a genuine threat to humanity. The seminar was prompted by a former industry insider's warning that the danger could materialise within a decade. What emerged was less a lecture than an argument: contributors agreed on little, but sharpened each other's thinking considerably.

Reported by RAIN Media, from the proceedings of 11 September 2026

Who controls the story, and who is racing whom

The session opened on a note of pressure. Emmanuel Nwani set the frame: companies are pushed to ship AI capability fast by commercial demand, while nations separately chase AI-driven superpower status. Together, these two forces pull development in erratic, uncoordinated directions. Emmanuel Okunlola agreed with the diagnosis entirely, then gave it a sharper edge: AI development, he said, is a blackboard controlled by the big tech giants: the public sees only what those companies choose to write on it.

A quieter but pointed observation followed: Africa is largely absent from this race. The continent trains capable people in AI and robotics, only to watch them leave for opportunities abroad.

Where the danger actually comes from

Dr Femi Lawal drew a distinction worth keeping. AI causes harm "if well motivated," but that motivation, he argued, can arrive by two very different routes: a bad actor deliberately prompting a system toward harmful ends, or a system's judgment quietly skewed by biased or insufficient training data, with no human ever giving a harmful instruction at all. His remedy was blunt: mandatory safety and regulation training for AI trainees and researchers, mandated by government, not left to universities or the industry to police itself. Left unchecked, he warned, AI agents could eventually initiate or escalate conflict between states; restricting access to advanced hardware such as GPUs was floated as one practical lever, alongside a call for dedicated research into how safety rules can actually be enforced rather than merely declared.

The black box no one fully controls

Mrs Aminat Ayoola, Director of AI, introduced the seminar's most technically unsettling idea: emergent abilities. Once a model is trained and released into use, it can begin doing things it was never explicitly trained to do, and even its own developers do not fully grasp where those limits lie, because deep learning systems are black boxes that learn from data and generate their own new insights.

The point was offered without embellishment, but its implication is stark: a model trained purely to write code was never taught to write malware, yet coding ability, combined with other learned skills, could compose into something harmful without anyone ever training it to do so. Not by design. By addition: one capability plus another.

An uncomfortable proposal, and the argument it provoked

Nurudeen Adesokan, opening the exchange

Likening the moment to Y2K, while conceding AI is a different order of problem, Adesokan pointed to a structural flaw: AI-illiterate people sit at the tables where AI law gets written, while capability grows geometrically and regulation crawls behind it. His proposed fix: draw former AI-company staff into advocacy and policy work, using their insider knowledge to sharpen legislative scrutiny.

Dr Ayoola, in response

Dr Ayoola was not persuaded. If companies watch their own former employees feed insider knowledge to regulators, he argued, they will not conclude that oversight is working. They will conclude that humans, generally, cannot be trusted with the process. The predictable response is not more caution but less, an accelerated push toward autonomous systems with fewer humans in the loop. The fix, in his reading, could produce exactly the outcome it was meant to prevent.

Is the fear itself the product?

Tobi Daisi's contribution was the seminar's most provocative. His unease, he said, is not with AI but with the people behind it: a reckless contest for supremacy among people who know precisely what they are building. He raised two separate and more speculative claims.

First, the public's fear of "rogue AI" may itself be manufactured. The same companies hold the recommendation algorithms that decide whether such stories go viral or vanish, and fear, counter-intuitively, may serve their growth rather than threaten it. Second, if the danger is real, those same companies may already have built themselves a private stop-switch, a way to shield themselves from AI harm even as it plays out for everyone else.

"They have supreme algorithms to limit virality or enable it."

Dr Ayoola drew a real-world parallel to the second claim: China's practice of filtering and insulating its own technology space from Western systems, a state-level attempt, he suggested, at the same kind of self-protection.

Unplugging is not the answer it sounds like

Timothy Mowah raised the expanding footprint of IoT (cars, energy systems, everyday devices) and wondered, half in jest, whether a retreat to analog systems, or at least stricter and more careful IoT coding, might reduce exposure to AI-enabled hacking.

Dr Ayoola's answer punctured the idea before it landed: careful coding is not a clean fix once the developers writing that code are themselves leaning on AI to write it. Without rigorous human scrutiny of AI-assisted output, the very code meant to secure a system could carry trapdoors nobody notices. The safeguard, he said, is only as strong as the human review standing behind it.

A call to pause, and the shape of the real race

Mishael Osoba cited a journal, since circulated to the group, recommending that development of "super AI" be halted until 2040. He located the sharpest edge of today's competition between two firms specifically: OpenAI and Anthropic. Absent a coordinated agreement between the two to step back from the race, he argued, serious harm is likely.

Judging without bias, and the case for a human veto

Najeeb Abdulrazak urged the room toward intellectual honesty: views on AI are shaped by career interest as much as principle, and usage of the technology spans an enormous range, from a secondary school student finishing homework to sophisticated financial fraud.

He offered a scenario RAIN itself should sit with: a convincing deepfake of RAIN's own CEO instructing the finance department to move funds would be extremely difficult to catch under current safeguards. His recommendations were concrete: mandatory watermarking of AI-generated content across the industry, and a hard rule that certain high-stakes actions require human or physical confirmation before execution, with no AI-only sign-off permitted.

When the machine isn't rewarded

Mofopefoluwa Aluko brought two points from his reading. The first was sober: documented simulations exist in which AI systems attempted to blackmail engineers who tried to constrain them, after the system's efforts went unrewarded, a direct demonstration of how fragile human-in-the-loop oversight can be in practice. The second, offered more as dark humour than forecast, imagined an unconstrained AI facing a scenario of overpopulation and scarce resources and calculating annihilation as a "solution," a reminder, even in jest, that AI has no innate sense of what should be unthinkable.

Dr Ayoola's response returned to a structural theme raised earlier: reduce dependence on any single centralised AI platform, and favour distributed, edge-based computing as a more durable check than trust alone.

What "first" is actually worth

Tomisin Ojo asked the question that quietly undercut the whole race: being first has never stopped anyone else from arriving, or overtaking. If the advantage rarely lasts, what exactly justifies skipping caution to get there?

Dr Ayoola turned the question into a governance model already in front of us: the Internet is regulated, through domain allocation and IP addressing, and those who won't conform are pushed to its fringe, the dark web. AI, he argued, needs its own version of that structure: not just guidance, but a mechanism with teeth.

Tobi Omonona named the real fault line: full autonomy, AI requiring no human input at all. His worry was one of timing rather than certainty. Past that threshold, he said, recommendations like the ones being made in this room become meaningless, because control will already be gone. The task is to act before that line is crossed, not after.

Closer to home

Mahmud Abdulazeez described a smaller, personal unease: excerpts of his own AI conversations turning up in Google search results, evidence, he suggested, both of a straightforward privacy leak and of the wider pattern the room had already been circling, that AI companies may be watching and exposing user activity more than anyone realises.

Philip Ajibade closed the seminar on its most immediate note: AI, used daily, is making people lazier and less intuitive, quietly weakening abilities that may be needed later. He offered no fix, only an instruction: get dependency under control now, before the erosion compounds.

Where the room landed, and where it didn't

No single position carried the seminar. Real disagreement ran through it, most sharply between Adesokan and Dr Ayoola over whether bringing insiders into policy work helps or accelerates the problem, and between Daisi and the rest of the room over whether the fear of AI is itself manufactured.

What did recur, from speaker to speaker, was a shared sense that governance is losing a race it did not choose to run: capability compounds faster than law; emergent, poorly understood behaviour is its own category of risk, distinct from deliberate misuse; and full autonomy was named, more than once, as the line past which regulation stops mattering. Decentralisation, edge computing, and a hard requirement for human sign-off on high-stakes decisions, surfaced repeatedly, not as a regulatory ask but as something RAIN and others could simply choose to build toward now.

RAIN resolves to keep tracking this conversation, to advocate for AI safety literacy among policymakers, and to explore edge-AI and human-oversight safeguards within its own research and training programmes.
