How this paper works

This is a submitted practical examination. There are two questions, and they are the same for every candidate. What differs is the scene. Ten scenes are printed below, Variant A to Variant J. Collect your assigned variant letter from Dr Ayoola, then answer both questions using only that scene. Work submitted on another candidate's scene earns no marks.

Notation and conventions

CoordinatesRight-handed Cartesian, in metres. Angles are positive anticlockwise.
RoomA closed polygon with vertices A, B, C, … The wall joining A and B is Wall AB, and so on. All walls are opaque.
LiDARA point sensor at L = (xL, yL) with heading θ measured anticlockwise from the +x-axis.
Scan angleα, measured from the LiDAR heading axis: negative to the right of the axis, positive to the left. The scan runs from −FOV/2 to +FOV/2 in steps of the stated angular resolution.
Global ray angleφ = θ + α
Reported ranged(α) is the smallest positive distance at which the beam meets a wall segment or an obstacle boundary.

A single LiDAR beam is a ray: it starts at L and travels in one direction only. In parametric form

x(t) = xL + t cos φ,    y(t) = yL + t sin φ,    t ≥ 0

Because (cos φ, sin φ) is a unit vector, t is the distance travelled along the beam. If a beam meets an obstacle at 2.40 m and a wall at 5.80 m, the LiDAR reports 2.40 m. The obstacle occludes that part of the wall: the wall is still there, but the sensor cannot see it. A LiDAR never reports a second return on one beam.

Question 1 — Distance-versus-angle plot

90 marks

For the scene assigned to you, determine the range returned at every scan angle across the stated field of view, and plot the result.

  1. Derive the equation of the LiDAR beam for a general scan angle α, and state clearly why t is restricted to t ≥ 0.
  2. For each scan angle, compute the candidate intersection distance with every wall segment, rejecting any intersection that lies behind the sensor or outside the segment.
  3. Compute the candidate distances to every obstacle. Derive and use the ray–circle quadratic for circular obstacles, and segment intersection for rectangular and square obstacles.
  4. Take the smallest positive candidate as the reported range, and tabulate α, φ, the object hit, and d to two decimal places.
  5. Plot d (vertical axis, metres) against α (horizontal axis, degrees). Label both axes with their scales and mark every plotted point.
  6. Annotate the plot: which angular ranges are wall returns, and from which wall; which are obstacle returns; what causes each sudden jump; which wall regions are occluded, and what range the LiDAR would have measured had the obstacle not been there.

You may work by hand, by program, or by a combination of the two. Whichever method you use, your table must cover every scan angle in your scene, and your written working must show the geometry and reasoning behind the numbers, not only the results.

Question 2 — Algorithm and code

10 marks

Write a program, or complete pseudocode, that generates the whole scan automatically for any scene of this kind. Python, C++, MATLAB or another language approved by the examiner may be used. It must:

  1. define the room geometry;
  2. define the obstacle geometry;
  3. define the LiDAR position;
  4. define the LiDAR orientation;
  5. generate the scan angles from the field of view and angular resolution;
  6. cast each ray;
  7. compute all intersections;
  8. select the nearest valid intersection;
  9. store the angle–distance pairs;
  10. plot the resulting scan.

Your code must handle the degenerate cases: a beam parallel to a wall (zero determinant), a negative discriminant in the circle test, and intersections with t ≤ 0.

What to submit

  1. The general ray derivation.
  2. Full working for the scan angles you calculated, showing every candidate intersection you considered and why the winning one was chosen. Marks are awarded for the candidates you reject as well as the one you keep.
  3. The completed angle–distance table.
  4. The plotted and annotated scan, on graph paper.
  5. Your algorithm or pseudocode, and if you wrote a program, the source code files together with the output they produce.
